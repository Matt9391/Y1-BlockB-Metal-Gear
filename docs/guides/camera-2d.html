    Guided exercise: Write the camera yourself (see Guided experiences). The world renderer accepts camera matrices; you will build the camera that supplies them and controls the view.

    What you will build#

    A side-scrolling level can be much wider than the game window. The camera chooses which part of that level is visible. Moving the camera should change the view without changing the positions of objects in the level.

    You will first build a camera that follows the player, then keep it within the level bounds and use it to convert mouse coordinates.

    World positions and screen positions#

    There are two coordinate spaces to keep separate:

    Space What a position means Examples Render function
    WorldA position relative to a fixed origin in the level.Player, enemies, bullets, terrain.on_render_world
    ScreenA position in window pixels, measured from the top-left.Score, health and menus.on_render_screen

    Without a camera, buz::world_renderer_2d also draws in window pixels. Once you set a camera, it transforms world positions into the view. buz::screen_renderer ignores the world camera, so the hud stays in place.

    For a camera without zoom or rotation, the conversion is a subtraction. If the view starts at world x = 4800, an enemy at world x = 5000 appears at screen x = 200:

    Keep the enemy's stored position at x = 5000. Apply the camera when drawing rather than changing the enemy's position to scroll the level.

    Step 2: give the renderer the camera matrices#

    buz::world_renderer_2d::set_camera takes a view matrix and a projection matrix. They perform two parts of the conversion:

    • The view matrix shifts world positions relative to the camera. Here, it translates them by -top_left().
    • The projection matrix converts the resulting pixel coordinates into the coordinate system used by the gpu. Build it with buz::make_orthographic_projection using the viewport size.

    Continue passing world positions to draw_sprite; the camera matrix applies the offset. Do not also subtract it in the draw call. Update the viewport size and projection when the window size changes.

    Check: Move only the camera. World objects should move across the window, while the hud drawn in on_render_screen stays in place.

    Interpolation moves the camera part of the way towards the target:

    A dead zone#

    A dead zone is a rectangle inside which the player can move without moving the camera. If the player leaves it, move the camera just enough to bring the player back to its edge. Adjust x and y independently.

    You can build the rectangle with buz::rect_from_center_size. Choose one coordinate space for the test: for example, make the rectangle in screen space and convert the player's position with world_to_screen.

    Check: With direct following, the player stays centered. With smoothing, the camera catches up. With a dead zone, small movements inside the rectangle leave the camera still.

    Following the player near the edge can reveal space outside the level. After updating the camera position, clamp its center so half the view fits on either side:

    Use buz::clamp for the valid range. If the level is smaller than the viewport on an axis, there is no position that keeps the whole view inside it. Center the level on that axis instead of passing an inverted range to clamp.

    Extensions#

    Zoom#

    Zoom changes how much of the world fits in the viewport. If zoom = 2 means drawing objects twice as large, the visible world size becomes viewport / zoom.

    For a positive zoom and no camera rotation, derive the view's top-left from that visible size. Translate the world by the negative top-left, then scale by zoom. Update both coordinate conversions too:

    Use the visible world size for level clamping. Test zoom, edge clamping and mouse picking together.

    Parallax backgrounds#

    Parallax makes distant layers scroll more slowly than the gameplay layer. Without zoom, scale the camera offset for each background:

    A factor of 1 uses the normal world offset, 0.5 scrolls at half that speed and 0 leaves the layer fixed relative to the window. Set the appropriate camera before drawing each layer.

    Pixel snapping#

    For pixel art, try rounding or flooring the camera's drawing offset to whole pixels. Fractional offsets can make the image shimmer as it moves. Keep mouse conversion consistent with the offset actually used to draw. If you add zoom, check alignment in screen pixels rather than assuming whole world coordinates will still align with them.

    Levels#

    Complete the basic level before adding the intermediate and hard features.

    Basic

    • Draw world objects through the camera in on_render_world and the hud in on_render_screen.
    • Make the camera follow the player; direct following is sufficient.
    • Keep the view within the level bounds when the level is large enough.

    Intermediate

    • Add frame-rate-independent smoothing or a dead zone.
    • Handle levels smaller than the viewport.
    • Convert mouse positions with screen_to_world before testing world objects.

    Hard

    • Add zoom while keeping clamping and mouse conversion correct.
    • Add parallax background layers.
    • Snap the drawing offset to pixels for crisp pixel art.

    See Drawing in 2D for drawing sprites and Utilities for buz::clamp and buz::rect.
