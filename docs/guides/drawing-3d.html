    Needs the 3D template. A 3D game derives from buz::game_3d and must link buz_renderer_3d.

    A 3D game draws its world with a buz::world_renderer_3d. buz::game_3d owns it and hands it to on_render_world every frame, the same way a 2D game gets its buz::world_renderer_2d. The hud is drawn as in a 2D game, with the screen renderer in on_render_screen (see Drawing in 2D).

    Your game only reaches the world renderer in on_render_world, so everything that uses it happens there: drawing, the camera and your own shaders below.

    Built-in shapes#

    Five shapes, each drawn at a position (its center) and a size, in the plain color of the material you give it:

    function shape
    buz::world_renderer_3d::draw_boxa box
    buz::world_renderer_3d::draw_spherea sphere
    buz::world_renderer_3d::draw_planea flat rectangle facing up, drawn from above only
    buz::world_renderer_3d::draw_cylinderan upright cylinder
    buz::world_renderer_3d::draw_octahedrontwo pyramids base to base

    Unlike sprites, the order you draw shapes in doesn't matter: nearer surfaces hide farther ones, because the engine keeps a depth buffer.

    With a few boxes and cylinders you can block out a whole level ("greybox" it) before any art exists; the level of sample_3d is built that way.

    See-through surfaces#

    Some surfaces have holes in them. On the first PlayStation, where every triangle counted, a railing or a fence was usually one flat quad whose texture has alpha 0 between the bars. Modern games often model the bars instead, but the same trick is still common for leaves, grass and wire fences. Set the material's alpha_cutoff to cut the transparent parts out: pixels whose alpha (texture times color) is below it are not drawn at all, so you see what is behind them, whatever order you draw in.

    Here m_railing_mesh is the flat quad and m_railing_texture the image with the bars, both loaded in the constructor like the crate above.

    Without a cutoff, alpha between 0 and 1 blends with what is already on screen, which makes water or glass see-through. Blending can only mix with what has been drawn before: an object behind the glass that is drawn after it does not show through. So draw blended surfaces after everything else.

    Your camera#

    Until you set a camera, buz::world_renderer_3d shows the world from a fixed spot up and to the side of the origin, looking at it. Your own camera is two matrices, handed to buz::world_renderer_3d::set_camera:

    • the view matrix moves and turns the world so the camera sits at the origin, looking down -z, with y up;
    • the projection matrix maps what the camera sees onto the window.

    The conventions your matrices have to follow:

    • The world is right-handed with y up: x to the right, y up and z towards the viewer. One unit is a meter.
    • Matrices are glm's: stored by column (m[column][row]), and a position is a column vector transformed as v' = M * v. The translation lives in the last column, m[3].
    • After the projection and the divide by w, x and y run from -1 to 1 across the window, and depth runs from 0 at the near plane to 1 at the far plane on every graphics api the engine uses. A projection made for OpenGL's -1 to 1 depth (glm's plain perspective) clips the wrong things.

    Your own shaders#

    The engine's shaders draw a mesh in one way: the texture times the color, lit by the one fixed light. That covers a lot, but not fog that swallows the far end of a corridor, or a guard's flashlight that lights a cone in front of him. For those you write your own shaders, and the engine draws your meshes with them.

    The engine's mesh shaders are shaders/diffuse_vs.hlsl (the vertex shader) and shaders/diffuse_ps.hlsl (the pixel shader). Copy both into new files in the same folder, for example fog_vs.hlsl and fog_ps.hlsl, and change the copies. They get the same vertices and the same per-draw constants as the originals, so until you change them they draw what the engine's shaders draw. The build compiles every .hlsl file in the shaders folder, and the end of the name says which kind of shader it is (_vs or _ps). What you write in them is up to you; the engine does not come with a fog or a spotlight shader.

    To draw with them, name them in a buz::surface_shading, without .hlsl, and hand it to buz::set_surface_shading:

    From then on every mesh and every built-in shape in this world renderer is drawn with your shaders, until you set other ones; the default buz::surface_shading is the diffuse pair. Setting the shading that is already in use does nothing, so you can call it every frame.

    Inside the shaders, the numbers the engine provides come in three blocks. The copies of the diffuse shaders already declare the first two:

    • The per-draw constants, at register(b0), change with every mesh you draw. world is the transform you drew the mesh with: it takes the mesh's vertices from object space into the world. The three normal_rows do the same for its normals, and lit, alpha_cutoff and base_color come from its material.
    • The frame data, at register(b2), comes from your camera: view_projection takes a position in the world to the screen, and camera_position is where the camera is in the world. It is the same for every mesh, so the engine sends it once a frame, and again after you call set_camera.
    • The shader data, at register(b1), holds numbers of your own; it is explained below.

    The diffuse vertex shader puts a vertex on the screen in two steps: mul(constants.world, ...) gives its position in the world, and mul(view_projection, ...) turns that into a position on the screen. Fog and a flashlight happen in the world, so their pixel shader needs that world position too. Add it to what the vertex shader hands the pixel shader, in both files:

    Both effects also need numbers that the engine doesn't know about: the fog's color and distance, the flashlight's position, direction and angle. buz::world_renderer_3d::set_shader_data hands them to your shaders. It copies them, keeps them for every draw that follows, in this frame and the next ones, until you set them again, and accepts at most 4096 bytes. Nothing checks that the struct and the cbuffer agree: if they don't, the shader reads the wrong numbers. hlsl packs a cbuffer in rows of four floats, and a member never straddles two rows. A float3 followed by a float share one row, but two float3s in a row take two rows, with an unused float after the first. Order the members so the C++ struct has the same gaps, or add padding floats where hlsl leaves them.

    Debug views#

    When a model looks wrong, it helps to see its triangles, or to see it without the light. Press F1 while the game runs to draw only the edges of every triangle. Press F2 to draw every surface as if its material were unlit, so you can tell a dark texture from a surface that faces away from the light. Press F4 to hide the debug lines described below, and again to show them.

    buz::game_3d handles these keys, so your game doesn't check them. To use other keys, see debug_controls in Getting started.

    Debug drawing#

    Debug lines draw the things your code works with but the player never sees: colliders, a guard's field of view, a patrol path, a ray you cast. When the game misbehaves, drawing them often shows the mistake right away, for example a collider that sits next to the model instead of around it.

    Debug lines are depth tested like every other surface, so a wall in front of a line hides it. They start switched on, and F4 hides all of them at once (see Debug views), so the calls can stay in your code instead of being removed when you are done debugging.
